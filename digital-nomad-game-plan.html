The Digital Nomad Game Plan: Cards, Banking & Taxes

A sequenced playbook for a US citizen going remote and living abroad — built to protect your income from unnecessary tax and fees, earn travel rewards, and turn a remote salary into real savings. Written around a Bali / SE Asia base, but the framework travels.

US Citizen No-income-tax state domicile Remote income via own LLC Bali → SE Asia
01 / The Big Picture

Three things that move the most money

Get these right and everything else is detail. Two of them are counterintuitive — the usual internet advice (and most YouTube videos) will steer you wrong.

01

Keep a solo LLC plain — don't rush to S-corp

Below ~$80–100k profit, S-corp's small savings get eaten by ~$2.5k/yr in filing costs — a net loss. "More write-offs" is a myth; you already get them. Revisit near ~$100k.

02

Bali is not tax-free

The nomad visa likely makes you an Indonesian tax resident — possibly from day one. "Nomad visa = no tax" is false. Real, unsettled exposure.

03

Set up everything before you fly

US banks and credit cards need a US address and your physical presence. Once you're abroad, opening them gets hard or impossible.

02 / Taxes

The tax plan

This is where the nomad videos go quiet — most don't cover US-citizen taxes at all, and the popular "0% tax via a Dubai company" play is built for non-Americans. The US taxes citizens on worldwide income no matter where they live. Here's what actually applies.

A useful baseline A one-time gift is tax-free to the person receiving it. Gifts aren't income to the recipient, and a gift from a US person needs no reporting form. (A gift over $100k from a foreign person needs an information form, but still isn't taxed.) Handy if a windfall is seeding your move.

The break you'll live on: the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)

  • As a US citizen working abroad, you can exclude up to $130,000 (2025) / $132,900 (2026) of your earned income from US income tax.
  • To qualify in year one as a country-hopper, you use the Physical Presence Test: 330 full days outside the US in a 12-month window. Track every day — including travel days and any trips home. A couple of careless US visits can blow it.
  • Income for work physically done overseas qualifies — it doesn't matter that you own the company that pays you, or that the company is based in the US.
  • You still file a return every year, even when the exclusion zeroes your income tax. The exclusion only exists if you claim it. Expat deadline auto-extends to June 15 (then Oct 15 on request).

The catch the videos skipped: the FEIE does not erase payroll tax

Social Security + Medicare (~15.3%) still applies to your earned income — a cost you'll owe no matter what. The FEIE only touches income tax. This is the whole reason the S-corp question matters — and why, at lower incomes, the answer is still don't bother.

The S-corp question: stay a plain LLC until you're bigger

A common pitch from accountants is "elect S-corp to save on payroll tax." It's right at higher incomes — but two things sink it for a solo earner under ~$80–100k profit:

  • The "more write-offs" pitch is a myth. A plain LLC (sole proprietor) already deducts every business expense an S-corp would. S-corp unlocks zero extra write-offs — its only edge is dodging the ~15.3% payroll tax on the slice you take as a "distribution."
  • At lower incomes that slice is too small to beat the overhead. Running an S-corp costs ~$2,000–$4,500/yr (payroll service + a separate business tax return + bookkeeping). The savings don't clear that cost.

Illustrative example, ~$70k profit (salary ~$48k / distribution ~$22k, 2025 single filer — approximate):

ScenarioPlain LLCS-corp (after ~$2.5k filing costs)
Living abroad (FEIE)~$9,900 total tax · ✓ simpler & cheaper~$8,800 tax, but −$2,500 costs = net ~$1,000–1,500/yr worse
Domestic (no FEIE)~$13,800 total tax · ✓~$12,500 tax, but −$2,500 costs = net ~$1,200/yr worse

Abroad, the FEIE already drops income tax to $0 for a plain LLC — so S-corp only buys payroll savings, which are smaller than the cost of running it. And a quirk called the stacking rule taxes an S-corp distribution on top of the excluded salary, adding income tax a plain LLC never pays. Approximations based on 2025 single-filer brackets; verified against IRS (FEIE, Form 2555) and CPA break-even guidance.

Bonus — a solo LLC owner skips the W-2 As a plain single-member LLC you don't pay yourself a W-2 salary at all — you take owner's draws and pay quarterly estimated taxes on the full profit. A W-2-to-yourself only exists in the S-corp world. One less thing to set up.
When S-corp flips to worth it It becomes worthwhile around ~$80k profit domestically, and ~$100k+ once you're abroad on the FEIE (the exclusion already zeroes income tax, so S-corp's payroll savings must beat both the filing costs and the new tax it creates on distributions). Below that: do nothing — no Form 2553, no payroll. And have any accountant model S-corp with the FEIE included — a domestic-only analysis overstates the benefit.

The Bali tax exposure — read this twice

Real, unsettled risk The official E33G Remote Worker Visa very likely makes you an Indonesian tax resident from arrival, not at day 183. Indonesian residents are taxed on worldwide income at 5–35%, and the "4-year foreign-income exemption" you may have heard about does not apply to E33G holders. A US–Indonesia tax treaty prevents true double taxation, but relief isn't automatic. The E33G requires ~$60k/yr income proof and is the clean legal path — but handle the tax side with an advisor. Get a paid cross-border consult before committing to a full year.

The "fly under the radar" tourist-visa route — two different radars

The US radar you can't hide from — but you don't need to. Your employer being in the US does not make your income US-taxable. What matters is where you physically do the work: working from Bali makes it "foreign earned income," so the FEIE legally wipes out your US income tax regardless of which visa you hold. Claim it openly — the IRS doesn't report your visa to Indonesia. You get the break either way.

The Indonesia radar — the real one, and it's tightening
  • C1 is a tourist visa: 60 days, extendable to 180 max (~6 months) — it can't legally cover a year and doesn't permit work.
  • Stretching a year means visa runs, now caught by facial-recognition autogates (≈ flagged by the 3rd consecutive reset).
  • The crackdown is real: ~2,600 foreigners deported from Bali in Jan–Jul 2025, plus a dedicated immigration task force since April 2026. Penalties run to deportation + a 1–10 year blacklist.
  • It doesn't even dodge the tax: staying >183 days makes you a tax resident regardless of visa — so "under the radar" = non-compliance, not a legal exemption.
Honest read: cheaper and lighter only if nothing goes wrong; can't cleanly cover a year; rising enforcement. The E33G is the durable path for a full year.

Keep your domicile clean & the housekeeping

  • Hold a no-income-tax domicile (Nevada, Florida, Texas, etc.): keep a driver's license, voter registration, vehicle registration, and a real residential address there. Sever ties to any prior taxing state. A virtual mailbox alone is not proof of domicile.
  • FBAR: if all your foreign accounts combined ever top $10,000 in a year, you file a free, simple report (Wise/Revolut balances may count — track them). The bigger FATCA form only kicks in at $200k+ for someone living abroad.
  • Foreign housing exclusion: exists, but SE-Asia rents are usually too low to clear the threshold — effectively zero benefit there. It matters in expensive cities.

Execution notes that matter
  • Open Schwab + Fidelity while you're physically in the US, with your home-state address on file. Schwab can close accounts with no US address. Don't use a mail-forwarding service as your address — banks increasingly flag those.
  • Don't rely on Wise to refund ATM fees — that's Schwab's job. Wise gives ~2 free withdrawals/month, then charges. So: Schwab card = pull cash; Wise = hold, convert, spend.
  • On the ground in Bali: skip a local bank account. Use BCA / BNI / Mandiri ATMs (BCA is the gold standard), withdraw the max (~$184/transaction), and always choose to be charged in rupiah (IDR), never USD. Bali is still cash-heavy — keep cash for warungs, scooters, markets.
Don't close anything before you go Keep your existing US bank accounts open — you generally can't reopen them from overseas. Leave your current bank as the income-landing + bill-pay + backup hub (just confirm your home-state address is on file). Don't spend abroad on a card that charges foreign fees — that's Schwab/Wise's job. You're adding Schwab + Wise alongside your bank, not replacing it.
04 / Credit Cards & Perks

Cards, points & lounges

If you have a thin credit file (one card, a year or two of history) and want lounges from the start, the key question is: do your cards stack? The honest answer drives the pick below.

"Stacking" — what it actually means Two strong cards from different banks (say, Chase Sapphire Preferred + Capital One Venture X) both earn rewards, but they're two different point currencies that can't be combined — so they don't truly "stack." A real stack = cards that pool into one bank of points. So pick a lane:

A clean starter stack — all-Capital One (lounges + one pooled currency)

Why this lane: lounges day one, both cards pool into a single mile bank, near-zero net annual cost once you travel, and Capital One is the friendliest premium issuer for a thin file. The all-Chase alternative (Sapphire Preferred + no-fee Freedom Unlimited) has better airline transfer partners but no affordable lounge card (Chase's is now $795) — so it loses the "lounges now" goal.

Rules to obey
  • Thin-file reality: a premium card is a reach on one year of history. Check Capital One's pre-approval first (no credit hit). If it's a no, get the no-fee Savor now to build, and reapply for Venture X in 6–12 months.
  • Don't close your oldest card. It's the foundation of your credit history — closing it shortens your file and dings your score right when you're building. Keep it open, use it occasionally.
  • Pay every statement in full. Goal is points + a thicker file, not debt. Carrying no balance keeps reported "utilization" low, which builds your score faster.
  • Use upfront moving spend (gear, flights, deposits) to clear sign-up bonuses — points on money you were spending anyway. Split it across each card's ~3-month window.
  • Carry a Visa/Mastercard always — Amex acceptance is weak across Bali/SE Asia.

Later (6–18 months), once your file grows

  • Add a business card (your LLC qualifies you) if you want into another ecosystem with better transfer partners. Watch Chase's 5/24 rule: it denies you if you've opened 5+ cards in 24 months.
  • Grab Global Entry / TSA PreCheck — it's usually credited by your travel card and speeds every US re-entry.
05 / A Windfall

Turning a windfall into a bedrock

A simple framework — not licensed investment advice, but the boring, standard answer that works.

  • Keep ~6 months of expenses liquid. In Bali that's cheap — comfortable living runs ~$1,600–2,400/mo, lean ~$900–1,500/mo — so a real emergency fund is only ~$10–15k.
  • Invest the rest through the brokerage that comes free with a Schwab account — low-cost, broad index funds. No extra accounts to open.
  • Your real wealth engine is your savings rate. US salary + Bali costs means you can bank a large share of your income every month. That monthly surplus, invested, will dwarf any one-time windfall. Protecting it from unnecessary tax (plain LLC + FEIE) and fees (the banking stack) is the whole game.

08 / Key Decisions

The calls only you can make

  • Structure: stay a plain LLC while profit is under ~$80–100k; revisit S-corp as income climbs — and model it with the FEIE, not domestic-only.
  • Visa route: the legal E33G (tax-resident from day one, ~$60k income test) vs. the cheaper tourist-visa route (can't cleanly cover a year, rising enforcement, doesn't dodge tax). The durable choice is the E33G — but it's a personal risk/cost tradeoff.
  • Card lane: lounges-now (Capital One) vs. best point value (Chase). Pick one and pool within it; don't split across both early.
