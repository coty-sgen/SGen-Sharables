The Discovery Playbook

How to walk out of any first call knowing the customer's business better than their own team does — and have them wanting your business by the end. Built from the calls that have closed for 12 years.

01 · The mindset

You're not pitching. You're qualifying each other.

Everything below only works if you actually believe this. The posture is what makes people open up.

It's mutual qualification

"This is about you proving we're a good fit today" — not you begging for the deal. Flip who's selling whom. Make them want your business.

Audit hard, say no more than yes

You take time upfront to understand the situation and make sure it's even worth engaging. Selectivity is what makes a yes mean something.

Be the advisor, not the vendor

"Whether we work together or not, here's something valuable for you." Give real homework and real diagnosis on the call. Trust compounds.

Honest broker at the end

"If we're not a match, I'll tell you exactly why — with real data — and tell you what I'd do if I were in your shoes." Removes the pressure that makes people guard information.

The thinking order — run every business through this

The whole call is reverse-engineering these four, in order:

  1. What is the sale?

    What's the actual value, and the different ways it can be presented/packaged?

  2. Who buys it?

    Which customer profiles, and what is each one actually buying from you?

  3. How & where would they buy?

    Where do those people already exist so you can place an ad or rank for what they search?

  4. How do you make them instantly want it?

    On the page, the video, the funnel — what flips them from looking to buying?

02 · The call arc

The order you move through it.

Don't interrogate. Move through these phases conversationally — each one earns the right to the next.

  1. Trade origin stories first

    Open with a peer-to-peer credibility exchange. Go first — share your own war story (the impossible rebuild, the $74M client) so they feel safe matching your candor. You're establishing you're a peer, not a salesperson.

  2. Frame the call in parts

    "Let's take this in two parts — first the specific thing, then I'll change gears and I'll have a bunch of questions." You're telling them up front you're going to dig, so the questions feel expected, not pushy.

  3. Dig in sequence

    Product → dream customer → hidden demand → economics → deal flow → sales machine → competitors → current marketing. (Full question bank in section 03.)

  4. Reframe what you heard

    Package it back into one strong conclusion in your own words. This is where you prove you understood — and surface the real problem they couldn't name.

  5. Close with homework + posture

    Reciprocity, the honest-broker promise, scarcity, and a concrete next step. (Section 04.)

03 · The question bank

The actual questions.

Lead with the bolded power-question, then follow the threads. The italics tell you what each one is really for.

A · The dream customer

"Who's your favorite customer? If you could wave a wand and they called every single time your phone rang — and it rang 10 times a day — who is that, and what are they buying from you?"

Why: Surfaces the dream profile AND the exact product they buy. If they narrow to "our ICP," push: "No — across everything you make money from, who's your favorite?"

"If you could raise the floor and only work with companies doing $5M+ a year, who would you keep — and who would you drop?"

Why: Reveals which segments drain energy vs. pay off, and the growth they actually want.

"What names would put you on the map? Who do you have to land to feel like you're hanging with the big boys?"

Why: Gets the aspirational logos (Tesla, NVIDIA, LVMH…) — that's your messaging and targeting north star.

B · The hidden demand (positioning)

"What do people think they want, when really they need your product?"

Why: Exposes the gap between customer language and what you actually sell — the root of every search/positioning problem. (Nobody googles "3D TV without glasses.")

"What need did they have — that they didn't have an answer for — that would lead them to you?"

Why: Finds the trigger moment and the words they use before they know you exist. Technique: paint the buyer's journey yourself ("someone on the innovation team says 'we need wow factor'…") and let them correct it.

C · The money

"What do they think they'll spend vs. what they ultimately spend? Where's the price gap between you and the cheap option?"

Why: Maps anchor price, real price, and where budget objections kill deals (2D vs 3D, "cut from the project").

"What's your customer acquisition cost? What's a typical order — smallest, biggest?"

Why: Tells you what they can afford to spend on marketing and how much a won deal is worth ($5–10k CAC, $30–50k orders, $1M installs).

"Where do you lose — and to what?"

Why: The honest answer (cheaper alternative, budget cut, long approval) tells you exactly what the funnel has to overcome.

D · The flow & business health

"What's your volume right now — orders a month, a year? How many clients last year? Best year ever? Where's the flow today?"

Why: Gives you a visual of the trajectory — growing, flat, or fell off a cliff — and why (caught a post-website-relaunch traffic drop this way).

"Walk me through your deal spectrum separately — the big slow ones vs. the small fast ones."

Why: Big deals (6mo–2yr, marquee logos) and small deals (quick, global, low-commit) need totally different funnels. Don't let them blur together.

E · The sales machine

"Do you have set packages, or is everything custom? How long to turn around a proposal, and what's your stack?"

Why: Shows how productized they are and where the bottlenecks/automation gaps are (HubSpot → PandaDoc → QuickBooks, 10–15 min proposals).

F · The competition → turn it into homework

"Who are your competitors? Who's beating you that shouldn't be? Where are they crushing you?"

Why: Builds the full competitive set and where they lose share of voice — your audit starting list.

"Do you ever see competitor ads in your own feed?" → then give the homework:

The move: "Start saving them — the ad and the landing page. Those LPs are hidden; the only way to find them is through the ad. A handful of those is a free market-research matrix — feed it to AI and pull the KPIs that make their spend work. Their A/B testing is already done for you." Valuable to them whether or not they hire you.

G · Current marketing reality & moat

"What do you have today — deck, brochures, video? Where do leads come from now — referral, channel, paid? What changed recently?"

Why: Inventories the assets you can repurpose and the channels in play. "What changed" surfaces the wound that brought them to the call.

"Where does IP — patents, trademarks, rights — fall in all this?"

Why: Finds the defensible moat and the proof points worth putting front and center.

04 · The reframe moves

Teach while you ask.

This is the secret weapon. You don't just collect answers — you hand back a sharper version of what they said. It proves expertise and gets them confirming or correcting, which surfaces even more.

Rename what they do, in your words

Reflect their business back with a crisp frame they haven't heard: "digital shelf-space management," "that integrator is really a glorified referral partner," "you need a LensCrafters website objective." Every reframe earns a "yeah, exactly" (confirmation) or a "not quite" (gold).

Throw a gentle challenge on the table

Don't just agree with everything — that reads as a vendor. Push back with reasoning: "I'll throw a challenge at you — I haven't seen a webinar succeed in years. Delivering your demo to every website visitor is less work and a better output." Disagreeing well is what makes them trust the times you agree.

The litmus test

By the end, they should feel like they learned more about their own business from you than they told you — and that you turned them down a path before they even asked for a proposal. That's when they start wanting your business.

05 · The close

End so they're chasing you.

  1. Give the homework

    One concrete, valuable task they'd benefit from "whether we work together or not." It makes the value real before any contract.

  2. Make the honest-broker promise

    "At the end of this, if we're not a match, I'll tell you exactly why — with tangible data — and I'll tell you what I'd actually do in your shoes." This is what unlocks the guarded information.

  3. Hold the scarcity posture

    Spend real time on how selective you are and how booked you are. "I'm trying to earn your business — but this is about you proving we're a good fit."

  4. Take the raw material, set the date

    "Don't even send the deck — just send me the transcript so I can process what's really being said." Then lock a specific follow-up date before you hang up.

06 · Quick reference

Never leave a discovery call without these.

The one-pager. If you only get through this list, you got the call.

  1. Who's your favorite customer — and what are they buying? (the wave-a-wand question)
  2. Who would you keep if you raised the floor to $5M+ clients?
  3. What names would put you on the map?
  4. What do people think they want, when really they need your product?
  5. What triggers someone to start looking for you?
  6. What do they expect to spend vs. what they actually spend? Where do you lose?
  7. What's your CAC, typical order size, and current order volume?
  8. Big slow deals vs. small fast deals — walk me through each.
  9. Packages or custom? How fast is a proposal? What's the stack?
  10. Who are your competitors, and who's beating you that shouldn't be?
  11. Where do leads come from today, and what changed recently?
  12. Where do your IP / moat / proof points sit?
